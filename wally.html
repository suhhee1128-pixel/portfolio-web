Wally

AI-Powered Behavioral Budgeting App

Role
UX/UI Designer · Product Designer
(UX Research · Interaction Design · UI Design · Frontend Collaboration)
Platform
Web / Mobile App
Timeline
Nov – Dec 2025 (6 weeks)

Overview

Wally is an AI-powered budgeting app designed to help users change spending behavior, not just track expenses.

While most personal finance apps focus on visualization and data logging, Wally reframes budgeting as a behavioral problem—providing emotionally aware, contextual guidance through conversational AI and insight-driven reports.

1. Problem Space

Why budgeting apps fail to reduce spending

Despite widespread adoption of budgeting tools, users continue to overspend.

Research revealed that:

  • Users can see their expenses but don't understand their patterns
  • Impulsive spending repeatedly breaks budgets
  • Dashboards feel overwhelming and judgmental
  • Insights lack clarity and actionable direction

Budgeting tools show what users spent, but rarely help them change how they spend.

2. Research & Insights

Desk Research

Key challenges in personal finance management:

  • Difficulty tracking expenses: 55.9%
  • Impulsive spending: 30%
  • Irregular income: 28%
  • Low financial literacy: 23.7%

Even with budgeting tools available, self-control and consistent financial habits remain difficult.

User Research

Surveys and usability testing with 27 users

Card sorting to understand how users mentally organize spending insights

Key insight: Users didn't want more data — they wanted clear explanations and guidance that felt human and supportive.

3. Persona

Habit-Seeking Spender — Sarah Kim

Age 23, student with limited income

High impulsive spending tendency

Strong motivation to save but lacks feedback structure

"I know I'm overspending, but I don't know when, why, or how to fix it."

This persona guided the product goal:

Help users recognize behavioral patterns before overspending becomes habitual.

4. Design Hypothesis

If users receive contextual, emotionally aware feedback about their spending patterns—delivered in a conversational and non-judgmental way—they will better understand their habits and be more likely to stay within budget.

This shifted Wally's direction from expense tracking to behavior coaching.

5. Solution Strategy

Core Design Principles

  • Behavior over raw data
  • Conversation over dashboards
  • Emotion-aware insights
  • Supportive, non-judgmental tone
  • Insight first, details later

6. Key UX Solutions

1️⃣ AI Chat-Based Behavioral Coaching

  • Conversational interface replacing static dashboards
  • Multiple AI personas to adapt tone (supportive, direct, playful)
  • Spending data translated into natural language explanations

Why this mattered: Users were more receptive to feedback when it felt like guidance rather than reporting.

2️⃣ Multi-Layered Spending Insights

Insights structured across:

  • Mood-based patterns (emotional triggers)
  • Time-based trends (when overspending occurs)
  • Overall summaries (long-term habits)

This helped users connect emotion, timing, and behavior—something traditional budgeting apps fail to surface.

3️⃣ Report Page as a Reflection Tool

The spending report page became the key reflection moment, where users interpret their behavior and decide whether to adjust future spending.

This made the report page a primary UX focus.

7. Usability Testing & Card Sorting

Research Method

To evaluate whether users could clearly understand and act on spending insights, I conducted usability testing focused on the report page.

Methods:

  • Open card sorting
  • Moderated usability testing
  • Post-task surveys

Participants: 27 users

Key Findings from Card Sorting

  • Users struggled to distinguish patterns from raw data
  • Emotional insights were mixed with transactional data, causing confusion
  • No clear entry point — users didn't know where to look first
  • Reports felt like spreadsheets, not explanations

Core insight: Users wanted the report page to tell a story, not present a data dump.

8. Report Page Redesign (Before → After)

Before

  • Flat information hierarchy
  • Equal visual weight across all data
  • Key insights buried among numbers
  • High cognitive load during reflection

After

Clear top-down hierarchy:

  • Key behavioral insight summary
  • Pattern breakdown (mood-based, time-based)
  • Detailed spending data

Visual grouping aligned with card-sorting results

Reduced noise and improved scanability

The report page shifted from a dashboard to a behavioral reflection tool.

11. Collaboration & Implementation

Although my primary role was UX/UI and product design, I collaborated closely on implementation:

  • Designed end-to-end UX flows and UI systems
  • Worked with engineers via GitHub (branches, PRs)
  • Participated in deployment via Vercel
  • Designed within real technical constraints (Auth, DB, Edge Functions)

This ensured design decisions were feasible, scalable, and production-aware.

12. Reflection

What I learned as a UX/Product Designer

Good features don't guarantee good usability
Behavior change depends on clarity, timing, and emotional framing.

Trust is a UX problem
Especially in financial products, tone and hierarchy matter as much as visuals.

Information architecture drives behavior
Organizing meaning is more impactful than adding features.

Wally reinforced my belief that strong UX sits at the intersection of human psychology, product strategy, and system constraints.
