Why does awareness of spending

not always translate into behavior change?

Wally is an AI-driven personal finance platform designed to help users change spending behavior, not just track expenses.

While most budgeting apps focus on dashboards and transaction logs, Wally reframes budgeting as a behavioral and emotional problem. By combining conversational AI with insight-driven reports, Wally helps users understand why they overspend and how to intervene—before overspending becomes habitual.

Problem

Why existing budgeting apps fail to change behavior

Despite widespread adoption of budgeting tools, overspending remains common.

Research

Research showed that the core problem wasn't data availability—but interpretation and action.

Challenges in Personal Finance Management

  • 55.9% Controlling Spending
  • 30% Irregular income

  • 23.7% Difficulty tracking expenses

Source: Academy Bank, 2025

Even with tools available, the gap between "knowing" and "doing" remains high due to behavioral barriers.

Design Hypothesis & Strategy

Design Hypothesis

If users receive emotionally aware, goal-based feedback at the right moment, they will be more likely to change spending behavior.

Core Strategy

  • Behavior over raw data
  • Conversation over dashboards
  • Insight first, details later

Key UX Solutions

1. AI-Based Behavioral Coaching

  • Conversational interface replacing static dashboards
  • Adaptive AI personas that adjust tone (supportive, direct, playful)
  • Spending data translated into natural language explanations
  • AI analyzes individual spending behavior in relation to user-defined goals and provides personalized, actionable guidance tailored to each user

2. Goal-Based Spending Report & Tracking

  • Goal-setting interface that allows users to define a target amount and time period
  • Automatic generation of a daily spending goal based on the user's selected target and timeframe
  • Daily visual tracking that clearly shows whether users stayed within or exceeded their daily goal
  • Circular summary visualization that provides an at-a-glance overview of spending distribution and progress
  • Report data directly connected to the AI coaching system, enabling the AI to deliver context-aware, personalized advice based on real spending behavior

Usability Testing

User Survey (n=27)

Period: Nov 28 – Dec 2, 2025
Method: Post-usage survey & qualitative feedback

To evaluate how users perceived Wally after hands-on use, I conducted a user survey focused on clarity, usefulness, and behavioral impact.

What Users Valued Most

  • 1. Actionable, behavior-shaping advice
    Users consistently praised the app for providing specific and practical spending advice, rather than generic financial tips. This reinforced the decision to position Wally as a behavioral coaching tool, not a passive tracker.
  • 2. Engaging and approachable UI
    Participants described the UI as “cute” and engaging, noting that the friendly visual tone made the experience feel less judgmental and more motivating—supporting long-term engagement.
  • 3. AI characters as a behavior driver
    78% of users reported that the AI characters helped them better control their spending. This validated the concept of using AI personas to deliver emotionally aware, supportive feedback instead of static reports.

“The mood-based insights were especially helpful.”
— User feedback

Areas for Improvement (Identified Through Survey)

  • 1. Desktop experience felt too mobile-oriented
    Some users noted that the phone-style layout felt unnatural on desktop, indicating the need for clearer platform-specific UI optimization.
  • 2. AI responses lacked clarity at times
    While users appreciated the tone, several mentioned that AI responses could feel noisy or overly verbose, highlighting the need for more concise, scannable explanations.
  • 3. Desire for more spending categories
    Users wanted additional and more granular categories, suggesting opportunities for category expansion and customization.
  • 4. Feature usage was not always intuitive
    Some participants were unsure how to fully use certain features, pointing to the need for lightweight onboarding cues and micro-copy guidance.

How These Insights Informed Next Steps

Based on the survey findings, I identified clear follow-up actions:

  • Refine desktop-specific layouts
  • Simplify and structure AI responses
  • Expand spending categories
  • Add short, contextual guidance to improve feature discoverability

These insights directly informed subsequent iterations of the report page and interaction design.

Card Sorting (n=5)

Period: Dec 1, 2025
Participants: 5 users
Method: Open card sorting focused on report page structure and feature comprehension

Iteration: Report Page Redesign

Before / After

The report page became the core reflection and decision-making moment. Based on card sorting findings, I redesigned the structure to improve clarity and reduce cognitive load.

Before:

  • Flat hierarchy
  • Equal visual weight across data
  • High cognitive load
  • Insights buried in numbers

After:

  • Clear top-down structure: Key behavioral insight summary, Pattern breakdown, Detailed spending data
  • Visual grouping aligned with card-sorting results
  • Reduced noise and improved scanability

Impact

  • Reduced report page depth to improve access to the tracker and full spending history
  • +20% increase in feature comprehension after card sorting usability testing

  • Designed UX flows, interaction logic, and UI systems with implementation in mind

Reflection

What this project reinforced

  • Behavior change depends on clarity, timing, and emotional framing
  • In financial products, trust is a UX and language problem
  • Information architecture drives behavior more than feature count

Designing behavior change requires aligning psychology, system constraints, and timing—not just good UI. Wally strengthened my belief that strong product design sits at the intersection of human psychology, product strategy, and technical constraints.
